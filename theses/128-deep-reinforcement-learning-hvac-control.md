# Deep Reinforcement Learning Approach for HVAC Control

**I. Vikström** — M.Sc. thesis, Aalto University, December 2020 (with TietoEVRY)

Part of [Reinforcement learning and control](../areas/reinforcement-learning-control.md).

Heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) accounts for roughly a fifth of global
energy consumption. There are two ways to reduce that: build machinery that draws less power,
or control the existing machinery better. This thesis takes the second. Most HVAC plant is
still controlled by simple rule-based schemes, which are reliable and easy to maintain but
blind to electricity price, weather and occupancy. Model-based alternatives need a calibrated building model that is rarely
available. The question here is whether an agent can learn a control policy from interaction
alone.

## The learning problem

Reinforcement learning does not decompose quite like supervised learning: there is no fixed
dataset and no labels. The useful substitutions are *experience* for data, *policy* for
model, and *reward* alongside loss. The last two are distinct objects that are easy to
conflate.

**Data points.** There is no corpus. Experience is generated by interaction: the agent acts,
an EnergyPlus building simulation responds, and the resulting transition is stored in replay
memory and sampled from later. One timestep is five simulated minutes, so a simulated year
is roughly 105,000 interactions. Agents were trained on Helsinki weather for 2013, a full
year so that all four seasons appear, and validated on the first half of 2014.

**Features.** The state vector read from the simulator each step: indoor temperature, current
heating rate, current cooling rate, number of occupants present, wind speed, wind direction,
and solar diffuse radiation rate — plus the Nord Pool electricity spot price, which is what
lets the agent learn to shift load towards cheap hours.

**Actions (in place of labels).** Not power output directly, but heating and cooling
*setpoints* between +15 °C and +25 °C at 0.2 °C resolution — 51 discrete actions per step.
The agent manipulates the thermostat, exactly as a human operator would.

**Hypothesis space.** A Deep Q-Network: an
[artificial neural network](https://dictionaryofml.org/terms/ann.html) approximating the
action-value function, which is what makes the high-dimensional state space tractable. The architecture, meaning the number and
width of hidden layers, was chosen by a coarse grid search, because a single
train-and-validate cycle took about four and a half hours. Exploration used linear epsilon decay.

**Reward, and loss.** These are two different things and the thesis keeps them separate. The
[reward](https://dictionaryofml.org/terms/reward.html) combines energy cost (power drawn ×
spot price at that moment) with a work-performance cost drawn from a model of how
productivity degrades as indoor temperature moves away from the comfortable zone. That
second term is what stops the agent from discovering that the cheapest building is an
unoccupiable one. The [loss](https://dictionaryofml.org/terms/loss.html) actually minimised
by gradient descent is the DQN temporal-difference error, the gap between predicted and
bootstrapped action values. It is not the reward.

## What it found

The best agent cut energy costs by 24.4% and 37.1% against two baseline control strategies
supplied by the simulator, while holding indoor conditions acceptable for simulated occupants.

The binding constraint is a systems problem rather than a learning one: communication
overhead between the reinforcement learning program and EnergyPlus meant simulating a single
year took nearly three hours, which is what limited the hyperparameter search. The reward
design is the part most likely to transfer to other building-control problems, specifically
the choice to price thermal discomfort in the same units as electricity.
